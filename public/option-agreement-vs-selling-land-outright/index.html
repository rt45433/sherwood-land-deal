Option Agreement vs Selling Land Outright: Which Is Right for You?

If a developer has approached you about your land, you will have heard two phrases: an outright sale and an option agreement. They lead to very different outcomes for you as the owner, and the right one depends on how much certainty you want, how long you are prepared to wait, and how much planning risk you are happy to carry. This page explains both in plain English so you can go into any conversation knowing what is being offered.

An outright sale, in short

You agree a price, the legal work is done, and on completion the land is the buyer's and the money is yours. If the land already has planning permission, the price reflects that. If it does not, the buyer is taking on the planning risk and the price will reflect that too - a developer buying without permission is paying for the chance of consent, not the certainty of it. I have written separately about selling land without planning permission and about how development land is actually valued, and both apply here.

The appeal is simple: a clean break, a known figure, and a completion date you can plan around. The drawback is that if the buyer later gets a better permission than anyone expected, that upside is theirs. Some outright sales include an overage clause, which gives you a further payment if a defined event happens after the sale, such as permission for more homes than assumed. It is worth asking about.

An option agreement, in short

With an option you do not sell the land now. You grant the developer the exclusive right to buy it within a set period, usually conditional on them obtaining planning permission. They pay you an option fee up front for that right, then spend their own money on the planning application, surveys and consultants. If permission is granted, they can exercise the option and buy the land at a price fixed by a formula in the agreement - very often a percentage of the land's market value with permission, assessed at the time. If they never get permission, the option expires and you keep the land and the fee.

The appeal is that someone else carries the planning cost and effort, and the eventual price is tied to the land's value with permission rather than a guess made years earlier. The drawbacks are real, though. Your land is tied up for the whole option period, which can be years. You have no control over how hard the developer pushes for planning, or whether they prioritise your site over others they hold options on. And at the end of it, the price is set by a valuation you may disagree with, after a discount you agreed before anyone knew what the permission would look like.

Who each route tends to suit

An outright sale usually suits you if you want the money within months rather than years, you are selling for a reason that will not wait, the land is a smaller plot where the planning question is relatively simple, or you simply do not want to spend years with a developer's name registered against your title. Most garden plots and single building plots fall into this category.

An option tends to suit you if the site is larger or more complicated, the planning position is genuinely uncertain and would be expensive to resolve, you are in no hurry and can afford to wait for a better outcome, and you are comfortable that the eventual price depends on a valuation process rather than a number in your hand today.

Neither is right or wrong. The honest aim is to match the route to your situation, not to the developer's preference.

Clauses to look at before signing an option

If you go down the option route, have a solicitor who regularly handles development land review it, not a general conveyancer. The points that matter most are the length of the option period and whether the developer can extend it, the longstop date after which it definitely ends, the formula for the purchase price and who appoints the valuer if you disagree, whether there is a minimum price, which costs the developer can deduct, what obligations they have to actually pursue planning, and what happens to the planning work if they walk away. A short clause on any of these can move a lot of money.

On tax, the two routes can be treated differently, and the timing of a disposal and any reliefs depend on your circumstances and how the land is owned. Please check with your accountant before you commit to either route.

Common questions about option agreements

Normally the developer. That is the main attraction of an option for a landowner - the buyer funds the planning application, the surveys, the consultants and the legal work to get permission, and you pay nothing towards it. Check the agreement says so in plain terms, and check whether any of those costs are deducted from the price later, because some agreements quietly recover them at completion.
Not in practice. An option gives the developer the exclusive right to buy for the whole option period, and it is usually registered against your title at the Land Registry so any other buyer would see it. You still own the land and can normally keep using it, but you cannot sell it to anyone else until the option either completes, is exercised, or expires. That is why the length of the option period matters so much.
The option lapses at its longstop date and you keep the land and the option fee. You will not receive the purchase price, because the sale was always conditional on planning. The land may be in a better or worse position than before - a refusal on the record can make it harder to sell, while a near miss with helpful officer feedback can make it easier. Ask the developer to hand over the planning work they did, and make sure the agreement obliges them to.
It can be different, not automatically better. The timing of when a disposal is treated as happening, how the option fee is treated, and which reliefs you might qualify for all depend on your circumstances and how the land is held. These rules change and the details matter, so speak to an accountant who deals with land transactions before you sign anything, whichever route you take.
