Selling a Garden Plot or Part of Your Garden

Selling off part of your garden can put a useful lump of cash in your pocket without you having to move house at all. Around Nottingham and the East Midlands, plenty of homes sit on plots big enough that a builder could fit another house alongside, and if that describes yours, it is worth finding out what the land is really worth. Here is how I look at garden plots, and the practical things you will need to sort out along the way.

When a garden is big enough to interest a builder

Not every large garden makes a plot. The three things a builder looks at first are access, services, and size. Access means a realistic way to reach the plot for building and for the future home, whether that is an existing driveway, a shared access, or frontage onto a road. Services means water, drainage, electricity and so on being close enough to connect without huge cost. Size means enough usable ground, once you allow for gaps to boundaries and to your own house, to actually fit a home with a bit of garden of its own. As a rough guide, a plot that could take a modest house with parking and a small garden is the sort of thing that gets a builder interested, but a long thin strip with no independent access rarely works. If you are not sure, send me the details and I will tell you honestly whether it stacks up.

The process, step by step

Selling part of your garden means creating a new, separate legal title for the plot, which your solicitor handles by splitting it off from your existing title. Alongside that, there is planning: either you obtain permission first, or you sell the plot to a buyer who takes on the planning themselves. And if your house is mortgaged, and most are, you will need your lender's consent to release the plot from their charge, because the garden forms part of the security for your loan. None of this is unusual, and it happens all the time, but it does mean a garden plot sale has a few more moving parts than selling a house. I am comfortable with all of it and can guide you through the order things need to happen in.

What a plot is realistically worth, and why

I value a garden plot the same way I value any development land, using the residual method. I start from what the finished house would sell for, then take away everything it costs to build and sell it, and the profit a builder needs, and what remains is what the plot can bear. Single plots carry a quirk worth knowing: building one house does not enjoy the economies of scale that a larger site does, so the build cost per home tends to be higher, and that pulls the land figure down a little compared with what you might expect from headline house prices. It is not me being mean with the number, it is simply how the maths works on a one-off build. As always, I will show you the workings so you can see how I got there.

The tax point, in general terms

There can be a tax angle when you sell part of your garden, and it is genuinely worth getting proper advice on. In broad terms, the relief that applies to your main home can, in many cases, also cover the sale of part of its garden or grounds, within limits, which can reduce or remove a tax bill. But whether it applies, and how much of the plot it covers, depends on your own situation, so I will not put a figure or a threshold on it here. Speak to your accountant before you commit, so you know where you stand and can factor any tax into your decision. It is one of those things that is much better sorted out up front than discovered afterwards.
