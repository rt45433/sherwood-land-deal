Selling Agricultural Land or a Paddock for Development

Maybe it is a paddock the horses left years ago, a few acres on the edge of the village, or a corner of a farm that has always been awkward to work. If you have had letters from land promoters, or a neighbour has sold a field for housing, you are probably wondering what your own land is really worth and who actually buys it. I buy land across Nottingham and the East Midlands, and this page is my honest answer to both questions.

What makes farmland or a paddock valuable for development

Agricultural value and development value are two different worlds. As grazing or cropping land, a field is worth what farming income can support. If housing could realistically be built on it, the value can be in a different league altogether. The gap between those two numbers is why land promoters send so many letters.

Whether your land has genuine development potential comes down to a few things. How close it sits to the existing edge of a town or village, whether it has safe road access or the realistic prospect of creating it, whether services like water and electricity are within reach, and how the local council's plan treats the area. A paddock tucked behind existing houses on the settlement edge can be a real prospect. Ten acres of open countryside with no access usually is not, however nice the view. I will tell you which one you have rather than letting you dream on it.

Your routes to a sale, compared honestly

Sell as agricultural land. If there is no realistic development angle, a local land agent or farm auction is the right home for it, and I will say so when that is the truth. There is no point dressing a grazing field up as a building plot.

Promotion or option agreements. A promoter or developer takes on the planning battle at their cost, and you sell once permission is won, sharing the uplift. These deals can work well on larger sites, but they tie your land up for years with no guaranteed outcome, and the contract terms decide everything. Never sign one without your own solicitor going through it line by line.

Get planning yourself, then sell. The highest price if it works, but you fund the planning application, the consultants, and the risk of refusal. For a small paddock the costs can eat a painful share of the gain. It suits sellers with time, appetite, and a strong site.

Sell outright to a buyer like me. I buy with or without planning permission, using the residual method, which starts from what the finished development would be worth and works backwards through build costs and profit to what the land itself can support. You get a clean sale on a known timescale, and I show you the full breakdown of how I reached the number rather than plucking a figure. The trade-off is the same as any direct sale. A buyer taking on the planning risk pays less than the land might fetch with permission already granted.

Things to sort out before you sell

Land sales fall over on paperwork more often than on price. If anyone grazes or farms the land, establish now whether that is an informal arrangement or a formal tenancy, because the difference matters legally and your solicitor will need the documents. Check your title for rights of way, covenants, or old overage clauses from when you bought. And think about tax before agreeing anything. Selling land that has been farmed, gifted, or inherited can have real tax consequences, and the right structure varies case by case. That is a conversation for your accountant, and it is worth having early.
